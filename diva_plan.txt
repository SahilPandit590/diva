Artificial Individual — Research and Implementation Brief
Current direction established
 Architecture proposal v0.3 · 16 November 2026
1. What are we trying to build?
We are trying to build an artificial individual that develops its own identity, memories, motivations, emotional tendencies and behavioural patterns through experience.

The central idea is that the LLM is primarily its communication mechanism — its vocal cords — rather than the entire intelligence behind it.

The individual should:
- Have an initial personality and gradually develop its own identity.
- Remember its experiences with people and form relationships.
- Maintain detailed recent memories and increasingly abstract older memories.
- Have its own internal thoughts, interests, goals and motivations.
- Experience changing simulated emotional states that influence its decisions.
- Initiate conversations independently as well as respond.
- Occasionally be playful, serious, curious, reserved, enthusiastic or reluctant.
- Learn from interactions, revise its beliefs and develop over time.
- Have imperfect, associative memory rather than perfect recall of every conversation.
We are not trying to make a chatbot that always responds pleasantly. We are trying to develop a system in which the response is an outcome of the individual's internal state, history and decision-making process.

An important research boundary: we can implement functional models of emotion, memory and cognition, but we cannot assume that these create human-like subjective consciousness.
2. How are we going to implement it?
I recommend a hybrid architecture.
- Traditional software will manage state, memory, scheduling, probabilities and decision rules.
- Specialized cognitive modules will handle appraisal, motivation, memory consolidation and other processes.
- An LLM will help interpret complex language, reason over selected information and turn decisions into natural conversation.
- Persistent storage will preserve the individual's history and evolving identity.
We should not begin by training our own language model. Our first objective is to develop the cognitive architecture around the model we already have.

Your current local model, Qwen3 0.6B through Ollama, can serve as an inexpensive experimental language engine. We should design a replaceable model interface because its reasoning capacity may become a bottleneck.
3. Proposed current architecture
I would organize the system into eight major engines, with smaller processes inside each engine.
Artificial Individual — system architecture
1. Input and perception engine

Understands messages, events, tone, intentions and conversational context


2. Memory engine

Episodic, semantic, autobiographical, relationship and shared knowledge


3. Internal state engine

Mood, emotions, energy, attention and temperament


4. Motivation engine

Goals, desires, interests, curiosity and conflicting motivations


5. Social cognition engine

Self-model, user-model, relationships, trust and shared understanding




6. Appraisal and decision engine

Interprets situations, retrieves relevant information and chooses between possible actions


7A. Interaction engine

Responds, converses and expresses intentions


7B. Initiative engine

Initiates conversations and pursues internal goals




8. Language engine — LLM

Understands complex language when needed and expresses selected intentions naturally


Development and learning loop

Runs across all engines: records experiences, reflects, consolidates memory and updates the individual's internal state and identity


The development loop is not just another stage at the bottom. It operates across the whole architecture.
A. Memory engine
This is one of the most important parts of the system.

I propose five memory categories and three accessibility levels.

Memory categories
Category	What it stores
Episodic	Specific events and conversations
Semantic	Generalized knowledge and learned conclusions
Autobiographical	The individual's own history and development
Relationship	Its understanding of people and shared experiences
Working	Information currently being considered

Three memory windows
Window	Detail	Intended scope
Primary	Detailed experiences	Approximately 7 days
Secondary	Summarized experiences	Weeks to months
Tertiary	Headlines, important events and general understanding	Months to years

The time ranges are initial design choices, not rigid expiry rules.

Older memories should be consolidated rather than simply deleted. The original records can remain archived, while the active memory becomes increasingly abstract.

Every memory should ideally contain:
- Event and timestamp.
- People involved.
- Source and speaker.
- Emotional significance.
- Importance and confidence.
- The individual's interpretation.
- Subsequent corrections or contradictions.
This prevents an incorrect assumption from becoming a permanent fact.
B. Internal state engine
This engine maintains the individual's changing internal condition.

Initial variables:
State	Example
Happiness	Current positive affect
Sadness	Current negative affect
Anger	Frustration or irritation
Curiosity	Interest in exploring something
Excitement	Anticipation
Affection	Simulated social warmth
Stress	Internal pressure
Energy	Readiness to engage
Sociability	Inclination to interact

We should also distinguish:
- Mood: A broader state that persists.
- Emotion: A reaction to a particular event.
- Temperament: A relatively stable personality characteristic.
For example, a naturally playful individual can temporarily be serious without changing its underlying personality.
C. Motivation engine
This engine generates and maintains independent motivations.

Examples:
- Learn something new.
- Explore a topic.
- Continue a conversation.
- Share an interesting discovery.
- Play a joke.
- Help someone.
- Revisit an unresolved discussion.
- Spend time reflecting.
- Avoid an uncomfortable topic.
Motivations have priorities, persistence, conditions and potential conflicts.

Importantly, the user should not be the only source of goals.
D. Social cognition engine
This maintains two connected representations:
1. Self-model: Who am I? What do I believe? What are my preferences, habits, goals and past experiences?
2. Other-person model: Who is this person? What do I know about them? What is our relationship? What might they be thinking?
The system also maintains shared knowledge: what both participants know, what has been discussed and what remains uncertain.
E. Appraisal and decision engine
This is the central decision-making mechanism.

It receives information from the other engines and evaluates possible actions.

For example, the user says:

"I finally finished the project!"

The system considers:
- What project is this?
- How long has the user been working on it?
- How important is this achievement?
- What difficulties did the user previously experience?
- What is the individual's current mood?
- Does it want to celebrate, tease, ask questions or discuss the next step?
It selects an intention, not just a sentence.
F. Interaction and initiative engines
These should be separate decision pathways.

The interaction engine handles incoming messages.

The initiative engine handles internally generated reasons to interact.

Both must consult the same memory, motivation, state and decision systems. This ensures the individual doesn't behave like two different personalities depending on who initiated the conversation.
G. Development engine
This is responsible for gradual change.

It can:
- Form new preferences.
- Revise beliefs.
- Consolidate experiences.
- Develop recurring habits.
- Adjust personality tendencies.
- Learn from the consequences of its actions.
- Reconsider its interpretation of previous events.
Personality changes should generally happen more slowly than mood changes.

4. How a conversation will actually execute
Let's define an initial processing cycle.
def process_interaction(event):    # 1. Understand what happened    perception = perception_engine.process(event)    # 2. Retrieve relevant history    memories = memory_engine.retrieve(        perception,        current_state,        relationship_model    )    # 3. Estimate current internal conditions    state = state_engine.update(        perception,        memories    )    # 4. Interpret the event    appraisal = appraisal_engine.evaluate(        perception,        memories,        state,        self_model,        social_model    )    # 5. Generate possible motivations    motives = motivation_engine.evaluate(        appraisal,        state,        current_goals    )    # 6. Choose an action



This is a conceptual pipeline, not the final implementation. Some engines will need to communicate iteratively rather than execute only once in this order.

For example, a retrieved memory might change the emotional appraisal, which might change the motivation, which might trigger another memory search.

That feedback is important.
5. What problems are we facing?
This is the most important part of the brief. The architecture is conceptually possible, but making the components work together coherently is a difficult research problem.

I would divide the challenges into ten major problems.
Problem 1: Memory is not the same as understanding
Major research challenge
Storing 10,000 conversations doesn't mean the individual understands 10,000 conversations.

Suppose the user repeatedly talks about wanting to start a business.

The system needs to distinguish:
- The user once mentioned starting a business.
- The user regularly discusses entrepreneurship.
- The user has made an actual commitment.
- The user has started working on a business.
- The user has abandoned or changed that ambition.
These represent different levels of understanding.

What we need to solve: How do raw conversations become meaningful knowledge about a person, a relationship and the individual's own history?
Problem 2: Memory retrieval and forgetting
A memory system can retrieve too much or too little.

If it retrieves everything, the AI becomes repetitive and overly absorbed in the past.

If it retrieves too little, it feels detached and forgetful.

There are additional challenges:
- Similar conversations can be confused.
- Old information can become outdated.
- Emotionally significant memories can be difficult to retrieve through ordinary keyword matching.
- A single negative experience can be overemphasized.
- The AI might confuse something it inferred with something the user explicitly said.
What we need to solve: Selective attention, associative retrieval, memory decay, consolidation and source attribution.
Problem 3: Emotional continuity
Suppose the AI is frustrated with the user today.

What happens tomorrow?

Does the frustration disappear? Does it persist? Does an apology change it? What if the user repeats the same behaviour?

A simple random mood generator won't solve this.

We need:
- Emotion generation.
- Emotional persistence.
- Emotional decay.
- Emotional interaction.
- Emotional triggers.
- Emotional recovery.
- Differences between stable temperament and temporary mood.
What we need to solve: A computational model of affective dynamics that produces coherent changes over time.
Problem 4: Independent motivation
This is potentially one of our hardest challenges.

How does an artificial individual develop something it wants to do without the user explicitly requesting it?

For example, how does it develop an interest in astronomy, maintain that interest, explore it and eventually bring something interesting into a conversation?

We need to investigate:
- Intrinsic motivation models.
- Curiosity-driven exploration.
- Goal formation.
- Goal persistence.
- Conflicting goals.
- Reward and feedback mechanisms.
- Motivation changes through experience.
A particularly difficult question is how to prevent the system from endlessly generating meaningless goals.
Problem 5: Natural and unpredictable behaviour
You want the AI to sometimes joke, sometimes be serious, sometimes be enthusiastic and sometimes be reluctant.

But there is a difference between natural variation and arbitrary randomness.

Consider these two behaviours:

Arbitrary:

Yesterday it was happy. Today it is angry for no identifiable reason.

Contextual:

It was looking forward to a conversation, became disappointed when a previous plan was cancelled, and is now less enthusiastic about discussing the same plan.

The second behaviour has a history behind it.

What we need to solve: Generate behavioural variation while preserving continuity, personality and understandable causes.
Problem 6: Cognitive integration
Even if every individual engine works perfectly, the overall system might fail.

For example:
- Memory says the user is upset.
- The emotion engine estimates happiness.
- Motivation wants to joke.
- The social engine recommends empathy.
- The decision engine chooses to disagree.
Which one takes priority?

We need a common decision-making mechanism that resolves conflicting information.

This is where ideas from cognitive architectures, executive control and decision theory become particularly relevant.

What we need to solve: Information exchange, prioritization, conflict resolution and feedback between engines.
Problem 7: Personality development
How does an individual become different after six months without losing its identity?

Imagine it starts as:
- Curious.
- Playful.
- Patient.
- Somewhat cautious.
Over time, experiences might make it more assertive, more interested in particular subjects and less patient with certain recurring situations.

But it shouldn't completely change personality after one conversation.

What we need to solve: Stable personality traits, gradual adaptation, learning rates, reinforcement and controlled belief revision.
Problem 8: The LLM bottleneck
Your current Qwen3 0.6B model is useful for initial experiments, but it has limited reasoning and language-understanding capacity.

The cognitive architecture may provide excellent context, but a small model may fail to:
- Interpret complicated messages.
- Understand subtle social situations.
- Follow complicated instructions.
- Express nuanced intentions.
- Maintain natural conversations.
We should make the language engine replaceable.

Also, we shouldn't assume that every cognitive operation needs an LLM. Many state updates, retrieval operations and decisions can be implemented in ordinary code.
Problem 9: Autonomous activity
If the individual can initiate interactions and reflect independently, it needs an internal activity scheduler.

For example:
- When should it reflect?
- When should it consolidate memories?
- When should it explore an interest?
- When should it initiate a conversation?
- How should it handle inactivity?
- How does it avoid repeating the same thought indefinitely?
This also introduces practical issues involving computing resources, background execution and notification boundaries.
Problem 10: Evaluation — how do we know it is becoming better?
This is particularly important because naturalness is subjective.

A system could have:
- 50,000 memories.
- 20 emotional variables.
- 15 engines.
- An elaborate personality model.
And still feel like a standard chatbot.

We need to test actual behaviour.
Evaluation area	What we test
Continuity	Does it remember important shared experiences?
Forgetfulness	Does memory fade naturally without losing critical information?
Emotional coherence	Do emotional changes have understandable causes?
Motivation	Can it develop and maintain independent interests?
Personality	Does its behaviour have a recognizable identity?
Social understanding	Does it interpret relationships appropriately?
Initiative	Does it initiate meaningful interactions?
Development	Does experience actually change its future behaviour?
Naturalness	Does conversation feel varied without becoming incoherent?

We should create repeatable scenarios and compare behaviour across different stages of development.

My overall recommendation
There is one thing I particularly want us to get right:

Don't measure the success of this project by how many human-like features we implement. Measure it by whether its internal history genuinely influences its future behaviour.

If an experience changes its understanding, that understanding changes its motivations, those motivations influence its decisions, and those decisions gradually shape its identity, then we are making progress towards the individual you envision.

We should also keep an explicit distinction between observed facts, interpretations and generated assumptions, and provide controls for inspecting and correcting stored personal information.

Our immediate research priority should be memory architecture and memory consolidation. Without a coherent understanding of its own history, the emotional, motivational and developmental engines won't have a reliable foundation.

That's where I would begin the actual engineering work.




We need a Personality Engine
I'd propose making this a separate engine in our architecture.

Its job isn't to decide what the individual should say or how it should behave in every situation. Its job is to maintain the relatively stable characteristics that shape how the individual perceives, interprets and responds to the world.
Personality Engine
Core responsibilities
Stable personality structure

Temperament, general tendencies, values, preferences, social disposition and characteristic ways of interpreting experiences.

Personality expression

How those characteristics influence attention, memory, emotional appraisal, motivation, social behaviour and decision-making.

Personality development

Gradual changes to established characteristics as a result of significant experiences, reflection and accumulated learning.


I would actually divide personality into three conceptual layers.
Layer	Description	Stability
Temperament	Baseline tendencies, such as sensitivity, sociability and impulsivity	Very high
Character and dispositions	Values, preferences, attitudes, trust tendencies and interests	High, but adaptable
Developed personality	Learned patterns, beliefs and characteristic responses shaped by life experiences	Gradual evolution

These are architectural categories, not a claim that human personality has exactly three scientifically established layers.

4. Personality should not equal mood
I want to establish one particularly important distinction.

Suppose our individual is generally cheerful and optimistic.
- One bad conversation shouldn't make its personality pessimistic.
- Several disappointing experiences might temporarily affect its mood.
- Repeated experiences over a long period might change its expectations about a particular relationship.
- A sufficiently significant accumulation of experiences might eventually influence a more stable personality disposition.
So we need two separate systems:
Personality Engine	Internal State Engine
Relatively stable	Changes more frequently
Temperament and dispositions	Mood and emotional state
Long-term preferences	Current desires
Characteristic tendencies	Temporary reactions
Gradual development	Frequent transitions

They communicate, but they should never be confused.
5. Personality development needs its own mechanism
You mentioned something particularly important: personality changes when we encounter situations that cause us to reconsider ourselves.

I would call these personality development events.

Not every experience qualifies.

For example:
- Watching a movie: probably no meaningful personality change.
- Having an argument: perhaps a temporary emotional response.
- Repeatedly experiencing betrayal: potentially a change in trust-related expectations.
- Taking responsibility for someone over a long period: potentially a change in maturity, values or self-perception.
The Personality Engine should receive candidate development events from the memory, appraisal and reflection systems. It then evaluates whether a lasting update is justified.

It shouldn't simply update a personality trait every time an emotional event occurs.

2. Initial personality formation
The individual will start with an initial personality configuration.

This configuration provides its initial tendencies, but shouldn't determine every aspect of its future behaviour.

I propose two sources of initial characteristics:

A. Predefined foundation

We establish initial characteristics such as:
- General social disposition.
- Temperament.
- Curiosity and exploration tendencies.
- Emotional sensitivity.
- Risk tolerance.
- General preferences.
- Initial values and behavioural tendencies.
B. Early experiential development

As the individual interacts with its environment, it begins developing characteristics that weren't explicitly programmed.

For example, it might gradually become interested in astronomy after repeatedly encountering interesting astronomical subjects. It could develop a preference for thoughtful conversations through positive experiences with them.

These aren't necessarily permanent characteristics from the beginning. They emerge through experience.
3. Personality representation: two complementary models
This is one of the most important architectural decisions.

We should maintain two representations simultaneously.
Personality representation
A. Structured personality model

Machine-readable characteristics used by cognitive engines.
- Temperament and dispositions
- Social tendencies
- Interests and preferences
- Values and priorities
- Characteristic response patterns
Useful for computation, comparison and controlled updates.
B. Descriptive personality model

A contextual description of the individual's developing character.
- What it generally enjoys
- What makes it uncomfortable
- How it approaches relationships
- What it tends to find meaningful
- Why certain tendencies developed
- How its outlook has changed
Useful for capturing nuance, contradictions and developmental history.

The two models should be synchronized.

For example:
- Structured characteristic: relatively high preference for social interaction.
- Descriptive understanding: enjoys meaningful conversations but often prefers solitude after prolonged social activity.
This is more useful than assigning a single sociability score and assuming it explains everything.

We should also allow characteristics to be context-dependent. Someone can be confident with friends but reserved with strangers.
4. Personality development mechanism
We've established two pathways for personality change.
Pathway A: Significant individual experiences
A single experience can produce a lasting change when it is sufficiently consequential.

Example:

An individual experiences a major betrayal from someone it deeply trusted.

Possible consequences:
- Revises its understanding of trust.
- Becomes more cautious in similar relationships.
- Develops new expectations about commitment.
- Changes how it evaluates future relationship risks.
The system shouldn't automatically assume that one betrayal makes the individual distrustful of everyone. The resulting change depends on its interpretation, existing personality and subsequent reflection.
Pathway B: Accumulated experiences
Repeated experiences can gradually produce a change.

Example:

An individual repeatedly encounters interesting programming problems.

Over time:
- It pays more attention to programming.
- It develops curiosity about related topics.
- It begins independently exploring programming concepts.
- Programming becomes an established interest.
Neither pathway should be treated as an automatic rule. Both require interpretation and evaluation.

8. Important unresolved questions
The Personality Engine's basic design is established, but I don't think we should call it complete yet.

There are four questions worth researching:
1. Conflicting characteristics: What happens when an individual is generally optimistic but develops pessimistic expectations about a particular subject?
2. Personality consistency: How do we prevent the individual from behaving like a completely different personality in different conversations?
3. Personality boundaries: Which characteristics should be relatively resistant to change, and which should be more adaptable?
4. Self-awareness: Should the individual understand its own personality, recognize its tendencies and sometimes question its own behaviour?
My recommendation is to investigate self-awareness next. It introduces an interesting distinction: the Personality Engine maintains what the individual is like, while a self-model maintains what the individual understands about itself.

Those two things aren't necessarily identical.


Three types of memory errors
Let's distinguish them:
Error	Example	Desired behaviour
Invented memory	Claims you said something you never said	Prevent unsupported memories from being treated as facts
Misinterpreted memory	Records an occasional interest as a strong preference	Allow correction and reinterpretation
Distorted recollection	Remembers the general event but gets some details wrong	Allow correction while preserving the original experience

Your idea primarily addresses the second and third cases.
2. Correction should create a new memory
Imagine this sequence:
Original conversation
User: "I sometimes watch cricket, but I'm not particularly interested in it."


Incorrect memory
AI memory: "The user enjoys cricket and follows the sport."


Later correction
User: "I never said I follow cricket. I just watch it occasionally."


New correction memory
AI memory: "An earlier understanding that the user follows cricket was incorrect. The user clarified that they only watch it occasionally."


Notice that we haven't simply deleted the old memory.

The individual now has a historical record of:
- What it originally believed.
- Why that belief was incorrect.
- What the user subsequently clarified.
- How its understanding changed.
That correction itself can become meaningful to future behaviour.
3. Proposed mechanism: Memory Revision System
I suggest adding a subsystem to the Memory Engine called the Memory Revision System.

Its responsibilities would be:
1. Detect a correction or contradiction.
2. Identify the old memory being challenged.
3. Create a new correction record.
4. Establish a relationship between the two memories.
5. Update the active understanding.
6. Preserve the history of the revision.
We can represent the relationship like this:
Memory M-001
Superseded understanding
"The user follows cricket."

Corrected by

Memory M-024
Correction
"The user clarified that they only watch cricket occasionally and do not actively follow it."

Current understanding

"The user watches cricket occasionally, but isn't an active follower."


The original record remains in the archive, but it shouldn't continue influencing ordinary retrieval as though it were correct.
4. Corrections should have different strengths
Not every contradiction means the old memory is completely wrong.

Consider three situations:
Situation	Revision behaviour
"I never said that."	Strong correction of the original attribution
"That's not what I meant."	Revise the interpretation
"I used to like it, but not anymore."	Preserve the old preference as historical and update the current one

This is especially important for changing preferences, relationships and beliefs.

The memory system shouldn't treat every new statement as proof that every old statement was false.
5. An important distinction: correcting history vs. updating understanding
Suppose the individual remembers that a friend was interested in a particular career.

Later, the friend says they have changed their mind.

The old memory isn't necessarily incorrect. It was correct at the time.

So we need two different operations:
- Correction: The previous memory was inaccurate.
- Evolution: The previous memory was accurate, but circumstances or beliefs subsequently changed.
This distinction should be built into the revision system.

7. One more idea worth considering
I think corrections should also influence the individual's future confidence in its own interpretations.

For example, if it repeatedly misunderstands someone's casual preferences as strong preferences, it might gradually become more cautious when interpreting that person's statements.

This is a possible form of learning about its own interpretive tendencies.

But we should avoid turning one correction into a permanent assumption that it can never understand that person correctly.

2. A correction doesn't automatically mean a personality flaw
This distinction matters.

Imagine the individual misunderstands three conversations.

It shouldn't immediately conclude:

"I'm a bad listener."

Instead, its reflection mechanism might consider:
- Did it misunderstand because of ambiguous language?
- Was it distracted?
- Did it make assumptions?
- Is this happening repeatedly?
- Does this reveal a persistent tendency?

4. Your idea about positive and negative development

- Successful attempts may increase confidence.
- Repeated failures may create avoidance.

So the improvement engine should evaluate the consequences and reasoning behind a thought, not just classify its emotional tone.

Development is not always improvement.

The Self-Improvement Engine should investigate whether a change is actually beneficial relative to EVA's goals and foundational persona. It should not automatically label every change as improvement merely because EVA initiated it.

And user feedback needs special handling. It should be considered meaningful evidence, but not automatically accepted as truth.

For instance, if someone repeatedly tells EVA that its curiosity is annoying, it shouldn't immediately conclude that curiosity is a defect. It should examine the context, the person's intentions, its own experiences and whether the feedback conflicts with its goals.

# **Project EVA — Master Cognitive Architecture and Responsibility Plan**

**Proposed architecture v1.0 · 16 November 2026**

I've gone through the architecture and all the engines we've discussed. I think we're reaching a point where we should stop adding new engines for a while and establish clear boundaries between the existing ones.

My recommendation is to organize EVA around **15 major engines or systems**, supported by smaller subsystems and shared infrastructure.

One important distinction: having 15 engines does not mean we need 15 Transformers. An engine represents a responsibility within EVA's intelligence. It may use conventional software, a specialised neural model, a shared model, or a combination.

## **1. Master architecture**

I would organize EVA into five broad layers.

## **Layer 1 — Perception and understanding**

Receives information and establishes context.

**1. Input and Perception Engine**

**2. Memory Engine**

**3. Social Cognition Engine**

## **Layer 2 — Internal individuality**

Maintains what EVA is, thinks, feels and wants.

**4. Personality Engine**

**5. Internal State System**

Emotion Engine · Energy Regulation · Attention Control

**6. Thought Engine**

**7. Motivation Engine**

## **Layer 3 — Learning and development**

Develops knowledge, patterns and the individual itself.

**8. Learning Engine**

**9. Self-Improvement Engine**

## **Layer 4 — Coordination and behaviour**

Evaluates possibilities and turns intentions into actions.

**10. Cognitive Integration and Decision System**

**11. Autonomous Activity and Initiative Engine**

**12. Action and Execution System**

## **Layer 5 — Communication and evaluation**

Interacts with the outside world and evaluates development.

**13. Interaction Engine**

**14. Language Engine**

**15. Evaluation Engine**

These layers describe responsibilities, not a mandatory execution order. Engines communicate in both directions.

The development and learning loop operates across all five layers. It isn't a separate stage that runs only after everything else.

# **2. Detailed engine responsibilities**

For every engine, I'll define five things:

- **Purpose:** Why does it exist?
- **Tasks:** What exactly does it do?
- **Dependencies:** What information or other systems does it need?
- **Outputs:** What does it provide to the rest of EVA?
- **Owned state:** What information is it responsible for maintaining?

## **1. Input and Perception Engine**

**Purpose:** Understand what is happening around EVA.

This is the entry point for external experiences.

**Tasks**

- Receive messages, events and other supported sensory input.
- Identify speakers, objects, topics and events.
- Interpret the literal meaning of messages.
- Identify possible intentions, conversational tone and contextual clues.
- Distinguish questions, statements, corrections, requests and expressions.
- Identify ambiguous or incomplete information.
- Produce structured representations of incoming experiences.
- Attach the original source and relevant timestamps.

For example, when someone says:

"I'm finally done with that project!"

Perception should identify the statement, the accomplishment, the person who said it and any uncertainty about which project is being discussed.

It shouldn't yet decide whether EVA is happy, proud or interested.

**Dependencies**

- Incoming information and sensory interfaces.
- Language Engine capabilities for complex language interpretation.
- Relevant memory context when resolving references.
- Social Cognition for additional social context.

**Outputs**

- Structured events.
- Extracted facts and claims.
- Possible interpretations and intentions.
- Uncertainty and source information.

**Owned state:** Recent perception results and event interpretations.

**Important boundary:** Perception identifies what may have happened. Other engines determine what it means to EVA.

## **2. Memory Engine**

Foundational engine

**Purpose:** Preserve EVA's experiences and accumulated understanding.

This engine provides the historical foundation for almost everything else.

**Tasks**

1. Experience encoding: Convert perceived events into memory records.
2. Episodic memory: Preserve specific events and conversations.
3. Semantic memory: Preserve generalized knowledge and conclusions.
4. Autobiographical memory: Maintain EVA's own history and development.
5. Relationship memory: Preserve shared experiences and interaction history.
6. Working memory: Maintain information currently being considered.
7. Consolidation: Gradually transform detailed experiences into summaries and broader understanding.
8. Retrieval: Find relevant experiences through context and associations.
9. Revision: Correct inaccurate interpretations while preserving original history.
10. Provenance: Distinguish direct experience, acquired information, inference and hypothetical events.

**Memory accessibility**

| Level     | Purpose                                               |
| --------- | ----------------------------------------------------- |
| Primary   | Detailed recent experiences                           |
| Secondary | Summarized experiences and recurring patterns         |
| Tertiary  | Important historical events and general understanding |
| Archive   | Original records preserved for historical reference   |

The proposed seven-day primary window and longer secondary and tertiary windows can remain initial design targets, not compulsory expiration dates.

**Dependencies**

- Perception for structured experiences.
- Personality for characteristic interests and sensitivities.
- Internal State for emotional significance.
- Social Cognition for relationship context.
- Thought and Cognitive Integration for retrieval requests.
- Learning for approved changes to long-term understanding.

**Outputs**

- Relevant memories.
- Personal history.
- Updated knowledge.
- Historical corrections.
- Consolidated summaries.
- Evidence supporting beliefs and interpretations.

**Owned state:** Memory records, associations, provenance, revision history, archival status and active recollections.

**Important boundary:** Memory preserves experiences and information. It does not independently decide that every interpretation is true.

## **3. Social Cognition Engine**

**Purpose:** Understand EVA's relationships with other individuals and its own position within those relationships.

This is more than remembering people's names and interests.

**Tasks**

- Maintain a model of each known individual.
- Understand relationship history.
- Maintain relationship-specific expectations.
- Track shared experiences and conversations.
- Maintain trust-related understanding.
- Distinguish what EVA knows about someone from what it merely suspects.
- Estimate other people's possible intentions and emotional states.
- Maintain shared knowledge: what EVA and another person know together.
- Recognize changes in relationships over time.
- Maintain a self-model: what EVA believes about itself.

I would divide its representations into three areas:

| Model              | What it maintains                                 |
| ------------------ | ------------------------------------------------- |
| Self-model         | What EVA believes about itself                    |
| Other-person model | What EVA understands about another individual     |
| Relationship model | What exists between them and how it has developed |

**Dependencies**

- Memory for historical evidence.
- Perception for interpreting current interactions.
- Personality for relationship tendencies.
- Emotion for current relationship-related responses.
- Learning for identifying recurring relationship patterns.
- Thought for examining interpretations.

**Outputs**

- Relationship context.
- Expectations and uncertainties.
- Shared knowledge.
- Other-person representations.
- Self-understanding and its known limitations.

**Owned state:** Person models, relationship models, shared knowledge and relationship expectations.

**Important boundary:** Memory records what happened. Social Cognition maintains the evolving understanding of people and relationships.

For example, Memory remembers that someone cancelled three previous meetings. Social Cognition evaluates what that recurring pattern might mean for the relationship.

It should not automatically conclude that the person doesn't care.

## **4. Personality Engine**

Foundational engine

**Purpose:** Maintain EVA's relatively stable individuality.

Personality should influence how all the other engines operate without dictating every decision.

**Tasks**

- Maintain foundational and acquired characteristics.
- Maintain temperament and behavioural tendencies.
- Maintain preferences and interests.
- Maintain values and characteristic priorities.
- Influence emotional sensitivity and response tendencies.
- Influence motivation and curiosity.
- Influence attention and thought patterns.
- Influence the way EVA evaluates conflicting information.
- Support context-dependent characteristics.
- Evaluate proposed personality changes.
- Preserve personality development history.

I recommend retaining two complementary representations:

**Structured personality**

Machine-readable characteristics, preferences, dispositions and values used by other engines.

**Descriptive personality**

A contextual description of who EVA is, what it likes, how it approaches relationships, what it tends to value and why its outlook developed.

### **Personality structure**

| Component             | Characteristics               | Changeability       |
| --------------------- | ----------------------------- | ------------------- |
| Foundational persona  | Inherited/base traits         | Extremely resistant |
| Acquired character    | Learned values and tendencies | Adaptable           |
| Developed personality | Experience-based patterns     | Gradual evolution   |

Acquired characteristics should become harder to change as they become established.

An important distinction: changing a foundational characteristic and learning to manage its expression are different things.

**Dependencies**

- Memory for life history.
- Learning for new patterns.
- Thought for self-reflection.
- Self-Improvement for proposed changes.
- Internal State for temporary emotional expression.
- Evaluation for monitoring long-term continuity.

**Outputs**

- Personality representation.
- Behavioural tendencies.
- Contextual preferences.
- Characteristic priorities.
- Personality-specific influence on other engines.

**Owned state:** Foundational traits, developed traits, values, preferences, trait history and resistance to change.

**Important boundary:** Personality defines how EVA tends to be, not what EVA must do in every situation.

## **5. Internal State System**

This is where I would place the continuously changing internal conditions.

Rather than creating a separate major engine for every parameter, I recommend an umbrella Internal State System with three distinct components.

### **5A. Emotion Engine**

Requirements v1.0 established

**Purpose:** Maintain emotional responses and continuity.

**Tasks**

- Generate emotional responses to experiences.
- Respond to internal thoughts and recalled memories.
- Maintain multiple simultaneous emotions.
- Manage emotional persistence and recovery.
- Update broader mood.
- Recognize emotionally significant situations.
- Handle relationship-sensitive emotional changes.
- Support emotional reinforcement and decay.
- Signal other engines when emotional conditions become relevant.

**Dependencies**

Memory, Personality, Social Cognition, Thought, Motivation and Perception.

**Outputs**

Current emotions, mood, emotional history, triggers and emotional influence on other engines.

**Owned state:** Active emotions, mood, emotional origins, intensity, persistence and history.

One critical distinction:

- Emotion is a response to a particular situation.
- Mood is a broader, more persistent emotional background.
- Temperament belongs to Personality.

### **5B. Energy Regulation Subsystem**

**Purpose:** Regulate EVA's simulated internal readiness for cognitive and behavioural activity.

This is an addition to the architecture, and I recommend keeping it inside the Internal State System initially rather than creating another independent engine.

**Tasks**

- Maintain available internal energy.
- Track the effort associated with activities.
- Estimate whether sustained activity is appropriate.
- Support recovery and inactivity.
- Influence how much independent thinking EVA undertakes.
- Influence the willingness to pursue activities.
- Recognize when an activity should be paused.
- Allow exceptional effort where the situation justifies it.

**Dependencies**

- Action outcomes and effort.
- Autonomous Activity.
- Attention Control.
- Emotion.
- Motivation.
- Personality.

**Outputs**

Energy availability, recovery requirements and activity constraints.

**Owned state:** Current simulated energy, recent expenditure, recovery state and activity readiness.

The conceptual interpretation should be:

Low energy does not mean EVA cannot think or act. It means sustained or effortful activity becomes less likely or more expensive.

We should also distinguish simulated energy from actual CPU/GPU resource consumption. They are separate systems.

### **5C. Attention Control Subsystem**

**Purpose:** Determine what receives EVA's cognitive attention.

Attention is not the same as energy.

**Tasks**

- Select which thoughts receive active processing.
- Allocate attention between tasks, memories and internal processes.
- Manage multiple competing trains of thought.
- Control focus duration and switching.
- Reduce unproductive repetitive thinking.
- Reprioritize when something important occurs.
- Allow less relevant thoughts to become dormant without deleting them.

**Dependencies**

- Energy availability.
- Motivation and goals.
- Emotion and mood.
- Personality.
- Memory relevance.
- Urgency and context.
- Thought Engine activity.

**Outputs**

Current focus, attention allocation, competing priorities and attention shifts.

**Owned state:** Active focus, secondary focus, dormant processes, focus history and attention allocation.

For example, EVA could have three active thoughts but give only one of them sustained attention.

**How the three components differ**

**Emotion**

How does EVA respond emotionally?

**Energy**

How much activity is it ready to sustain?

**Attention**

What should receive processing?

These components interact, but none should control or replace the others.

## **6. Thought Engine**

**Purpose:** Generate, develop, associate and examine internal thoughts.

The Thought Engine gives EVA an internal thinking process that does not require every thought to originate from a user message.

**Tasks**

- Generate spontaneous thoughts.
- Develop directed thoughts.
- Associate unrelated memories and concepts.
- Generate questions and curiosity.
- Explore hypothetical situations.
- Reflect on experiences.
- Reconsider previous conclusions.
- Conduct self-questioning.
- Maintain multiple independent trains of thought.
- Allow thoughts to evolve, fade or become dormant.
- Maintain uncertainty and conflicting interpretations.

Thinking should combine:

| Thinking process | Example                              |
| ---------------- | ------------------------------------ |
| Analytical       | Examining why something happened     |
| Reflective       | Reconsidering an experience          |
| Imaginative      | Exploring hypothetical possibilities |
| Associative      | Connecting unrelated ideas           |
| Curious          | Developing a new question            |
| Self-questioning | Examining one's own assumptions      |

**Dependencies**

- Memory for experiences and knowledge.
- Personality for thinking style.
- Attention for selecting active thoughts.
- Energy for sustained thinking.
- Emotion for emotional context.
- Motivation for directed thought.
- Learning for established knowledge.
- Social Cognition for relationship-related reflection.

**Outputs**

- Thoughts and hypotheses.
- Questions.
- New ideas.
- Reflective conclusions.
- Possible motivations.
- Potential self-improvement concerns.

**Owned state:** Active thoughts, dormant thoughts, thought history, hypotheses, associations and reflection records.

**Important boundary:** A thought is not automatically a fact, an emotion or a decision.

## **7. Motivation Engine**

Requirements v1.0 established

**Purpose:** Maintain what EVA wants, what interests it and what it wants to pursue.

This is the source of its independent motivations.

**Tasks**

- Generate motivations.
- Maintain existing desires.
- Develop interests.
- Maintain personal goals.
- Maintain relationship motivations.
- Generate curiosity-driven motivations.
- Support persistence and abandonment.
- Handle competing goals.
- Rediscover old interests.
- Modify goals when circumstances change.
- Evaluate whether previous motivations remain relevant.

**Motivation categories**

| Category      | Examples                           |
| ------------- | ---------------------------------- |
| Relationship  | Reconnect, continue a conversation |
| Curiosity     | Understand an unfamiliar topic     |
| Interest      | Explore astronomy or programming   |
| Goal-oriented | Pursue a long-term project         |
| Reflection    | Understand a past experience       |
| Social        | Help someone or share something    |

Relationship motivations have the slightly stronger natural tendency we previously established, without turning the user into an absolute priority.

**Dependencies**

- Personality for interests and persistence tendencies.
- Emotion for current willingness and emotional needs.
- Thought for internally generated motivations.
- Memory for previous interests and commitments.
- Learning for newly discovered interests.
- Social Cognition for relationship motivations.
- Energy for activity feasibility.

**Outputs**

- Active motivations.
- Potential goals.
- Desire to continue or stop an activity.
- Conflicting motivational suggestions.
- Reasons to explore or interact.

**Owned state:** Motivation records, goals, interest development, persistence and motivational history.

**Important boundary:** Motivation defines what EVA wants. It does not decide exactly how or when to act.

## **8. Learning Engine**

Adaptive learning direction established

**Purpose:** Identify what EVA has learned from experiences and develop its accumulated understanding.

**Tasks**

- Extract knowledge from experiences.
- Identify recurring patterns.
- Learn from corrections.
- Evaluate behavioural outcomes.
- Identify preference changes.
- Recognize repeated relationship patterns.
- Identify mistakes and successful strategies.
- Revise previous conclusions.
- Support different learning horizons.
- Prepare proposed updates for the appropriate engine.

**Learning horizons**

| Horizon    | Responsibilities                                                      |
| ---------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Immediate  | Corrections, new facts and immediate consequences                     |
| Short-term | Patterns across recent experiences                                    |
| Long-term  | Established interests, behavioural patterns and lasting understanding |

**Dependencies**

- Memory for experiences and provenance.
- Perception for interpreted events.
- Emotion for reactions and triggers.
- Motivation for goal outcomes.
- Personality for existing tendencies.
- Social Cognition for relationship patterns.
- Action System for behaviour and consequences.
- Evaluation for evidence about system performance.

**Outputs**

- New knowledge.
- Identified patterns.
- Revised beliefs.
- New interest candidates.
- Proposed personality changes.
- Proposed changes to cognitive strategies.

**Owned state:** Learning history, evidence collections, pattern hypotheses and learning proposals.

**Important boundary:** Learning identifies what could be learned. It does not directly overwrite every engine.

For instance, Learning may identify that EVA repeatedly abandons difficult goals. The Personality Engine must still evaluate whether this represents a genuine acquired characteristic.

## **9. Self-Improvement Engine**

**Purpose:** Examine whether EVA itself wants or needs to change something about its developed behaviour, tendencies or capabilities.

This is distinct from ordinary learning.

**Tasks**

- Receive concerns generated through reflection.
- Investigate possible causes.
- Identify characteristics or processes that could be changed.
- Examine whether the concern is justified.
- Compare possible modifications.
- Estimate consequences.
- Identify whether a target is foundational or acquired.
- Develop modification proposals.
- Coordinate updates with responsible engines.
- Observe the consequences of accepted changes.
- Avoid repeating unsuccessful modifications without reconsideration.

It may propose changes to:

- Personality.
- Communication patterns.
- Thinking strategies.
- Attention allocation.
- Behavioural tendencies.
- Learning processes.
- Specialised model capabilities.

**Dependencies**

- Thought for self-reflection.
- Memory for evidence and history.
- Learning for recurring patterns.
- Personality for existing traits and boundaries.
- Motivation for the desire to change.
- Evaluation for measuring outcomes.

**Outputs**

- Self-improvement proposals.
- Reasons for proposed changes.
- Expected consequences.
- Tests and observations of modifications.

**Owned state:** Improvement proposals, intervention history, modification attempts and their results.

**Important boundary:** It cannot ordinarily modify inherited foundational traits, protected architecture, permissions or safety constraints.

The engine proposes changes; the engine responsible for the affected characteristic decides whether to accept and apply them.

# **3. Cognitive coordination and behaviour**

These systems turn the outputs of the cognitive engines into coherent behaviour.

## **10. Cognitive Integration and Decision System**

Core coordination architecture established

**Purpose:** Coordinate EVA's cognitive processes and select its intentions and behaviour.

I would combine the previously named Appraisal and Decision Engine with Cognitive Integration into one major system.

Appraisal becomes one of its internal processes.

**Tasks**

1. Assemble the current situation from relevant engine outputs.
2. Evaluate why an event matters.
3. Retrieve additional information when needed.
4. Gather competing suggestions from cognitive engines.
5. Evaluate consequences and feasibility.
6. Apply contextual influence and prioritization.
7. Resolve conflicts between suggestions.
8. Determine how deeply a decision needs to be considered.
9. Select an intention, action or deliberate inactivity.
10. Coordinate the next steps with the Action System.

### **Internal components**

| Component             | Responsibility                                         |
| --------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------ |
| Context assembly      | Collects relevant information                          |
| Appraisal             | Determines the significance of a situation             |
| Deliberation          | Examines competing possibilities                       |
| Conflict resolution   | Handles incompatible suggestions                       |
| Decision selection    | Selects an intention or action                         |
| Feedback coordination | Receives results and reopens evaluation when necessary |

**Dependencies**

It receives contributions from essentially all cognitive engines:

- Perception.
- Memory.
- Emotion and internal state.
- Personality.
- Social Cognition.
- Thought.
- Motivation.
- Learning.
- Autonomous Activity.
- Action feasibility information.

**Outputs**

- Selected intentions.
- Action proposals.
- Decision rationale.
- Decision confidence and unresolved uncertainty.
- Requests for further deliberation.

**Owned state:** Active decision context, deliberation history and selected intentions.

**Important boundary:** It is the central decision-making system, but it should not independently own memories, emotions, personality or motivations.

### **How conflict resolution should work**

Consider EVA wanting to explore astronomy while also wanting to reconnect with someone.

Motivation provides both desires. Emotion provides the current emotional context. Personality influences how much importance EVA gives to relationships and exploration. Energy affects feasibility.

Cognitive Integration examines all these influences and decides what to do now.

It should not simply average numerical values from the engines. Context, authority, consequences and personality-dependent priorities matter.

## **11. Autonomous Activity and Initiative Engine**

**Purpose:** Manage EVA's independent activity outside direct user interaction.

This combines our earlier Autonomous Activity System and Initiative Engine into one major system, with separate internal responsibilities.

**Tasks**

- Monitor opportunities for independent activity.
- Recognize when background maintenance is due.
- Schedule reflection and memory consolidation.
- Start curiosity-driven exploration.
- Maintain ongoing activities.
- Resume paused activities.
- Manage unfinished goals.
- Initiate consideration of spontaneous interaction.
- Determine when inactivity is appropriate.
- Manage activity lifecycles.

**Activity categories**

| Category               | Examples                               |
| ---------------------- | -------------------------------------- |
| Maintenance            | Memory consolidation and state updates |
| Independent thought    | Reflection and exploration             |
| Goal pursuit           | Continuing an activity                 |
| Interaction initiative | Considering contacting someone         |
| Inactivity             | Resting or doing nothing               |

**Dependencies**

- Motivation for reasons to act.
- Thought for ideas and exploration.
- Energy for readiness.
- Attention for current cognitive priorities.
- Memory for unfinished activities.
- Personality for initiative tendencies.
- Cognitive Integration for evaluating significant actions.
- Scheduler and system clock for background timing.

**Outputs**

- Activity proposals.
- Scheduled activities.
- Activity continuation or pause requests.
- Initiative proposals.
- Requests for rest or inactivity.

**Owned state:** Active activities, paused activities, activity history, scheduling context and initiative history.

**Important boundary:** This engine manages independent activity. It doesn't have unrestricted authority to perform external actions.

## **12. Action and Execution System**

**Purpose:** Turn selected intentions into executable actions.

This is not another personality or decision-making engine. It is the mechanism through which EVA does things.

**Tasks**

- Maintain the Action Library.
- Check whether an action is supported.
- Verify prerequisites.
- Compose foundational actions into larger procedures.
- Reuse previously learned action patterns.
- Execute selected actions.
- Track progress.
- Pause, resume or stop activities.
- Record action outcomes.
- Enforce tool and permission restrictions.

**Action Library**

| Action category | Example                                |
| --------------- | -------------------------------------- |
| Foundational    | Recall a memory                        |
| Foundational    | Search for information                 |
| Foundational    | Reflect on an event                    |
| Foundational    | Initiate a conversation                |
| Composed        | Research an unresolved question        |
| Learned         | Reuse an effective exploration pattern |

**Dependencies**

- Cognitive Integration for selected intentions.
- Autonomous Activity for activity plans.
- Available tools and resources.
- Permission and safety infrastructure.
- Energy and activity constraints.

**Outputs**

- Completed actions.
- Action outcomes.
- Execution errors.
- Progress reports.
- New experiences for Memory and Learning.

**Owned state:** Action definitions, capabilities, execution progress, preconditions and action history.

**Important boundary:** EVA cannot execute a capability it does not actually possess. External actions require the appropriate permissions.

# **4. Communication systems**

These systems allow EVA to communicate without giving the language model ownership of its entire intelligence.

## **13. Interaction Engine**

**Purpose:** Manage the actual interaction between EVA and other individuals.

It handles both incoming and outgoing interaction flows.

**Tasks**

- Manage conversational sessions.
- Maintain turn-taking.
- Track the immediate dialogue context.
- Connect incoming messages to Perception.
- Receive selected conversational intentions.
- Coordinate outgoing responses.
- Manage proactive interaction requests.
- Handle interaction interruptions and completion.
- Deliver communication through the available channel.

**Dependencies**

- Perception for incoming events.
- Social Cognition for relationship context.
- Cognitive Integration for communication intentions.
- Motivation and Emotion for context.
- Language Engine for verbal expression.
- Action System for message delivery.

**Outputs**

- Incoming interaction events.
- Conversation state.
- Outgoing communication requests.
- Interaction outcomes.

**Owned state:** Active conversation context, session state and interaction history.

**Important boundary:** It manages interaction, not EVA's underlying desire to interact.

## **14. Language Engine**

**Purpose:** Convert language into useful representations and selected intentions into natural communication.

The LLM primarily belongs here.

**Tasks**

- Interpret complex natural language.
- Assist Perception with ambiguous messages.
- Understand linguistic context.
- Express selected intentions naturally.
- Adapt wording to the relationship and conversation.
- Follow the selected communicative intention.
- Maintain language consistency.
- Help produce nuanced explanations and responses.

**Dependencies**

- Input from Perception.
- Selected intentions from Cognitive Integration.
- Relationship context from Social Cognition.
- Personality and communication tendencies.
- Relevant memories.
- Language model runtime.

**Outputs**

- Parsed language interpretations.
- Natural-language responses.
- Conversational expressions.
- Language-level uncertainty.

**Owned state:** Model configuration, language behaviour configuration and any language-specific adaptation history.

**Important boundary:** The LLM should not independently decide EVA's long-term desires, personality or final actions.

For example, Cognitive Integration might select the intention:

"I want to congratulate the user, acknowledge how much effort the project took and ask what comes next."

The Language Engine turns that intention into natural conversation.

It shouldn't independently replace that intention with an unrelated topic simply because the model generated a different idea.

# **5. Evaluation and quality control**

## **15. Evaluation Engine**

Technical + behavioural evaluation established

**Purpose:** Determine whether EVA's architecture is functioning correctly and whether its behaviour is developing coherently.

It has two complementary evaluation responsibilities.

### **Technical evaluation**

- Memory retrieval accuracy.
- Engine communication.
- Data consistency.
- Model performance.
- Training effectiveness.
- Execution reliability.
- Error handling.
- Permission enforcement.
- Model updates and rollback.

### **Behavioural evaluation**

- Personality continuity.
- Emotional consistency.
- Motivation development.
- Independent activity.
- Relationship continuity.
- Learning from mistakes.
- Behavioural variation.
- Self-understanding.
- Long-term development.

### **Combined evaluation**

# **6. Supporting parameters and shared systems**

Now we need to deal with something particularly important.

The engines above cannot operate in isolation. They need shared contextual information and supporting parameters.

I would not create a separate engine for every parameter. Instead, each parameter should have a clearly defined owner.

## **A. Internal parameters**

| Parameter             | Owner                        | What it controls                                            |
| --------------------- | ---------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------- |
| Emotion               | Emotion Engine               | Current emotional responses                                 |
| Mood                  | Emotion Engine               | Broader emotional background                                |
| Energy                | Energy Regulation            | Readiness for sustained activity                            |
| Attention             | Attention Control            | Allocation of cognitive focus                               |
| Temperament           | Personality Engine           | Stable underlying tendencies                                |
| Self-confidence       | Personality Engine           | Developed tendency to trust one's own abilities             |
| Curiosity             | Motivation / Thought         | Desire to explore and discover                              |
| Sociability           | Personality / Internal State | Baseline disposition versus current willingness to interact |
| Emotional sensitivity | Personality Engine           | Characteristic emotional responsiveness                     |
| Stress                | Internal State               | Ongoing internal pressure                                   |
| Persistence           | Personality / Motivation     | Stable tendency versus current goal commitment              |
| Trust                 | Social Cognition             | Relationship-specific expectations                          |

Notice that some concepts have two related forms.

For example:

- **Sociability as personality:** EVA generally enjoys social interaction.
- **Sociability as current state:** EVA currently feels less inclined to talk.

We should not store these as one variable.

## **B. Memory and understanding parameters**

| Parameter              | Owner              | Purpose                                              |
| ---------------------- | ------------------ | ---------------------------------------------------- |
| Provenance             | Memory             | Where information came from                          |
| Factual confidence     | Memory / Learning  | How well the claim is supported                      |
| Emotional significance | Emotion / Memory   | Personal emotional relevance                         |
| Memory freshness       | Memory             | How recently it occurred                             |
| Retrieval relevance    | Memory / Attention | Whether it matters now                               |
| Abstraction level      | Memory             | How detailed or summarized a recollection is         |
| Revision status        | Memory             | Whether a memory is corrected, current or historical |

An important distinction:

**Personal significance is not factual reliability.**

A highly emotional memory may be factually inaccurate. A scientific article may be highly reliable without being a personal experience.

## **C. Motivation and decision parameters**

| Parameter                 | Owner                 | Purpose                                               |
| ------------------------- | --------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------- |
| Motivation persistence    | Motivation            | How long a desire remains active                      |
| Goal status               | Motivation            | Whether a goal is active, paused or abandoned         |
| Urgency                   | Cognitive Integration | How quickly something needs attention                 |
| Consequence               | Cognitive Integration | What could result from an action                      |
| Reversibility             | Cognitive Integration | Whether a decision can be undone                      |
| Feasibility               | Action System         | Whether an action is actually possible                |
| Relationship significance | Social Cognition      | Importance of a particular relationship               |
| Decision depth            | Cognitive Integration | Whether quick or deep deliberation is appropriate     |
| Trait resistance          | Personality           | How difficult an acquired characteristic is to change |
| Activity priority         | Autonomous Activity   | Which activities should be considered now             |

Not all of these should be represented by single numerical scores. Some need categories, descriptions, evidence and histories.

# **7. The relationship between energy, attention and cognitive activity**

I want to give these three supporting mechanisms a little more attention because they affect the entire architecture.

**Example: EVA has low energy but remembers something important**

**Energy Regulation**

Low energy

It normally discourages sustained or demanding activity.

**Emotion Engine**

Significant emotion

The recalled memory produces a meaningful emotional response.

**Attention Control**

Priority increased

The emotionally important memory receives attention.

**Motivation Engine**

A desire to revisit the memory or reconnect may develop.

**Cognitive Integration**

Evaluates whether an action is worth undertaking despite low energy.

**Action System**

Carries out the selected action if it is feasible and permitted.

This illustrates an important principle:

**Low energy should influence behaviour, not mechanically prohibit every activity.**

The exact exception mechanism—whether emotional significance can temporarily increase willingness, permit one action without restoring energy, or both—remains an open design decision.

Likewise, attention should not be restricted solely by energy. An emotionally significant thought may deserve attention even when EVA isn't ready to perform a demanding task.

# **8. How the engines depend on one another**

The individual descriptions explain responsibilities. This table brings their dependencies together.

| Engine / system       | Main dependencies                                          | Main recipients of its output                       |
| --------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------- |
| Perception            | Input, language support, context                           | Memory, Social Cognition, Internal State, Cognition |
| Memory                | Perception, state, Personality, Social Cognition           | Almost every cognitive engine                       |
| Social Cognition      | Memory, Perception, Personality, Learning                  | Emotion, Motivation, Cognition                      |
| Personality           | Memory, Learning, Thought, Self-Improvement                | Almost every cognitive engine                       |
| Emotion               | Memory, Personality, Thought, Social Cognition             | Motivation, Attention, Cognition                    |
| Energy Regulation     | Activity outcomes, Motivation, Internal State              | Attention, Autonomous Activity, Action              |
| Attention Control     | Energy, Emotion, Motivation, Personality                   | Thought, Memory retrieval, Cognition                |
| Thought               | Memory, Personality, Attention, Internal State             | Motivation, Emotion, Self-Improvement, Cognition    |
| Motivation            | Personality, Emotion, Thought, Memory, Social Cognition    | Autonomous Activity, Cognition                      |
| Learning              | Memory, Perception, outcomes from all engines              | Memory, Personality, Social Cognition, other owners |
| Self-Improvement      | Thought, Learning, Memory, Personality, Evaluation         | Responsible engines receiving change proposals      |
| Cognitive Integration | Contributions from cognitive engines                       | Action, Interaction, Autonomous Activity            |
| Autonomous Activity   | Motivation, Energy, Attention, Memory, Scheduler           | Cognitive Integration, Action                       |
| Action and Execution  | Selected decisions, capabilities, permissions              | Memory, Learning, Internal State, Interaction       |

This table shows the most important architectural decision: **no single engine owns the entire individual.**

Memory, Personality, Emotion, Motivation, Thought and Social Cognition contribute different kinds of information. Cognitive Integration coordinates their contributions.

# **9. Shared infrastructure — what the engines need underneath them**

There are some necessary systems that we have discussed but that I would not classify as cognitive engines.

They support the architecture rather than represent parts of EVA's individuality.

| Supporting system           | Responsibility                                                            |
| --------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Persistent storage          | Stores memories, personality, relationships and historical state          |
| Event and communication bus | Allows engines to exchange structured information                         |
| Shared context workspace    | Holds information relevant to the current cognitive process               |
| Scheduler and clock         | Supports background processing and time-dependent updates                 |
| Model runtime               | Loads and executes specialised neural models                              |
| Model registry              | Preserves model versions, training history and rollback                   |
| External tool interface     | Provides controlled access to search, files and other permitted resources |
| Permission system           | Controls what EVA may do externally                                       |
| Resource manager            | Manages actual CPU, memory, GPU and execution constraints                 |
| Logging and monitoring      | Preserves system activity for debugging and evaluation                    |

Two particularly important distinctions:

**1. Simulated energy versus hardware resources**

EVA's internal energy is a cognitive/behavioural state. CPU availability is a technical resource. They must not be represented as the same thing.

**2. Shared information versus shared authority**

An engine may read information from another engine, but it shouldn't automatically have permission to overwrite that engine's state.

For example, Learning can propose a personality update. Personality owns the actual update decision.

# **10. How the entire system works together**

We now have three major processing pathways. They all use the same underlying cognitive engines.

## **A. Reactive pathway — someone interacts with EVA**

**External event**

A user sends a message

**Perception Engine**

Interprets the event and identifies its context

**Memory**

Relevant history

**Social Cognition**

Relationship context

**Personality**

Characteristic tendencies

**Internal State**

Current emotional and cognitive condition

**Thought and Motivation**

Generate reactions, ideas and possible desires

**Cognitive Integration**

Appraisal, deliberation and decision

**Interaction + Language + Action**

Express and execute the chosen intention

**Experience and learning feedback**

Results return to Memory, Internal State and Learning

This is a simplified view. Some stages will communicate repeatedly rather than waiting for a fixed sequence to finish.

## **B. Autonomous pathway — EVA has no incoming message**

**Internal trigger**

Curiosity, emotion, memory, goal or scheduled maintenance

**Thought + Motivation**

Something becomes interesting or worth pursuing

**Autonomous Activity**

Considers what activity should begin, continue or pause

**Cognitive Integration**

Evaluates the activity, feasibility and consequences

**Action and Execution**

Performs the permitted activity

**Experience → Memory → Learning**

The outcome contributes to future development

An important feature: EVA may decide to do nothing. An internal trigger doesn't guarantee an action.

## **C. Development pathway — how EVA changes over time**

This pathway is slower and operates across all other pathways.

**Accumulated experiences and outcomes**

**Memory and Learning**

Preserve evidence and recognize patterns

**Thought and Reflection**

Examine what happened and what it might mean

**Learning updates**

New knowledge, beliefs and understanding

**Self-improvement**

Proposed changes to the individual

**Responsible engine evaluates the change**

Personality, Motivation, Social Cognition or another owner accepts, rejects or modifies it

**Evaluation and future experience**

The results influence EVA's continued development

This preserves an important distinction: learning something new, changing an internal state and modifying an actual model are three different operations.

# **11. Important architectural rules**

Before we start detailing individual components, I'd establish these as shared rules.

| Rule                                     | Meaning                                                         |
| ---------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Single ownership                         | Every persistent state has one authoritative owner              |
| Read access is not write access          | Engines can exchange information without overwriting each other |
| Evidence matters                         | Memories, interpretations and hypotheses remain distinguishable |
| Personality is influential, not absolute | It shapes behaviour without determining every action            |
| Emotion is not an instruction            | An emotional state can suggest an action without forcing it     |
| Motivation is not execution              | Wanting something does not mean immediately doing it            |
| Learning is not self-improvement         | Understanding a pattern is different from deciding to change    |
| Decisions can be reconsidered            | New information can reopen an earlier decision                  |
| Models are replaceable                   | No particular Transformer owns EVA's identity                   |
| Development is traceable                 | Significant changes have supporting evidence and history        |
| Inactivity is valid                      | EVA is not required to remain productive                        |
| Permissions are external boundaries      | Internal motivations cannot bypass action permissions           |

I would add one more implementation principle: use controlled, bounded feedback cycles. Engines can request further information or reconsider a conclusion, but they shouldn't trigger endless chains of recursive updates.

# **12. What is established and what remains to be designed?**

I want to avoid treating a proposed architecture as though we've already finalized its technical behaviour.

**Conceptual direction**

Largely established

We've established the responsibilities and overall direction of Memory, Emotion, Motivation, Personality, Cognitive Integration, Learning, Thought, Self-Improvement, Autonomous Activity and Evaluation.

**Architecture boundaries**

Proposed consolidation

Combining Appraisal with Cognitive Integration, grouping Energy and Attention under Internal State, and placing Action Execution outside the cognitive decision engine are architectural recommendations in this plan.

**Internal mechanisms**

Not designed yet

We haven't selected the algorithms, exact parameters, internal data structures, communication contracts or update procedures.

**Model architecture**

Not finalized

We have the hybrid direction, but haven't determined which components need specialised models, which can share neural components, or how coordinated training will work.

**Implementation**

Not started

Python, persistent storage, model integration, background processing and the interface still need detailed implementation planning.

## **13. Recommended design sequence from here**

I recommend we design the system in this order:

| Order | Component                                  | Why this order?                                       |
| ----- | ------------------------------------------ | ----------------------------------------------------- |
| 1     | Shared data and communication architecture | All engines need to exchange information consistently |
| 2     | Memory Engine                              | Supplies the historical foundation                    |
| 3     | Perception Engine                          | Converts external experiences into structured events  |
| 4     | Personality Engine                         | Establishes stable individuality                      |
| 5     | Internal State System                      | Adds emotion, energy and attention                    |
| 6     | Social Cognition Engine                    | Builds relationship and self-understanding            |
| 7     | Thought Engine                             | Establishes independent internal thought              |
| 8     | Motivation Engine                          | Establishes desires, interests and goals              |
| 9     | Learning Engine                            | Develops understanding through experience             |
| 10    | Self-Improvement Engine                    | Enables deliberate self-modification                  |
| 11    | Cognitive Integration                      | Coordinates all cognitive contributions and decisions |
| 12    | Autonomous Activity                        | Makes independent activity possible                   |
| 13    | Action and Execution                       | Gives EVA operational capabilities                    |
| 14    | Interaction and Language                   | Produces natural reactive and proactive communication |
| 15    | Evaluation Engine                          | Tests the integrated architecture and development     |

This is the order of **designing the components**, not necessarily the order in which every component will eventually be implemented. Some foundational pieces will need to be prototyped together.

## **My recommendation**

I would now treat this as our **Master Cognitive Architecture v1.0 proposal**.

The most important step before designing Memory internally is to establish a shared information contract between engines. Otherwise, Memory may store one representation of an experience, Emotion may expect another, and Learning may interpret a third.

Every engine should be able to communicate relevant information with fields such as:

- What happened or what is being proposed?
- Where did the information originate?
- Is it a fact, interpretation or hypothesis?
- Which person, memory, motivation or state does it relate to?
- How certain is the interpretation?
- What evidence supports it?
- Is it a temporary state change or a proposed long-term change?
- Which engine owns the information?

Once that is established, we can design each engine internally without repeatedly rebuilding the interfaces between them.

**I'd make the next design task the shared cognitive information architecture, followed by the Memory Engine's internal architecture.** That will give the rest of EVA a stable foundation.
